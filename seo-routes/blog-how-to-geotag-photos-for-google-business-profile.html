Geotagging Google Business Profile Photos: Uses and Limits

By FreeGeoTagger · Updated September 26, 2026

Adding GPS to a business photo can help organize your own image archive. It is not a documented shortcut to a higher Google Business Profile ranking. Google describes local ranking in terms of relevance, distance and prominence; its published guidance does not establish an EXIF GPS ranking benefit.

Editorial correction, September 26, 2026: an earlier version of this guide claimed that Google reads every EXIF field and gives geotagged photos extra ranking weight. Those claims were unsupported and have been removed.

Start with a useful photo, not a metadata promise

A clear entrance photograph helps a visitor recognize your shop. A recent interior image helps them decide whether it meets their needs. Neither requires changing the recorded capture location. Do not assign a storefront coordinate to a stock image, off-site product photograph or customer home to imply it was taken somewhere else.

Google's local ranking guidance is the reference for profile optimization. Complete the profile accurately, maintain opening hours and respond to reviews. There is no basis here for promising that a particular number of tagged photos will change rankings.

A worked example: sorting photos from two branches

Suppose your archive has entrance and interior photographs from Branch A and Branch B. A shared camera clock or similar filenames does not tell you which branch a file belongs to. First separate the files using your shoot notes. If a photo already contains GPS, read it before changing anything. Only assign a location when you can establish where it was taken.

  1. Keep an original folder and work on copies. Group images by the actual capture site.
  2. Open GPS Finder and inspect one file from each group. Record any existing coordinates; do not overwrite good data simply for consistency.
  3. If GPS is missing, open the geotagging tool, select that branch's photos and enter a known location. Check the map and the sign of both coordinates.
  4. Download the edited copies and reopen them in EXIF Viewer. Compare the stored latitude and longitude with your intended values.
  5. Keep the branch name in your folder structure too. A website may remove metadata, and a filename or archive index is easier to search than invisible EXIF fields.

Expected result: your local copies contain the locations you entered. This exercise does not test Google's ingestion pipeline, prove capture location, or measure ranking changes.

Check Google's upload requirements separately

FreeGeoTagger's supported input formats and 20 MB limit are not Google's upload rules. Google's photo guidance lists JPG or PNG, 10 KB to 5 MB, a minimum of 250 by 250 pixels and a recommended 720 by 720 pixels. Images should be clear, well lit and represent reality.

For example, a 12 MB photo may open in this tool but still need resizing before you submit it to Google. Resize a copy, then inspect that final file if keeping EXIF is important to your archive. Do not assume metadata survives every editor or upload service.

When to leave GPS out

For a service-area business, a job photo may expose a customer's address. Obtain permission and consider removing location metadata from the public copy. You can keep a private, accurately tagged archive without publishing the same information. Also inspect the visible image for house numbers, faces and vehicle plates: deleting EXIF does not hide them.

Questions this tool cannot answer

It cannot verify your Business Profile, resolve a suspended listing, approve an image, or tell you whether Google retains a particular EXIF field. A locally verified geotag and an accepted Google photo are different outcomes. Use the profile's own photo status and official help for upload problems.
