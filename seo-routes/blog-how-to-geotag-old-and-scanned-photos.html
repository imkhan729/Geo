How to Geotag Old Photos and Scanned Pictures

By FreeGeoTagger · Published September 26, 2026 · Updated September 26, 2026

Quick answer: You can add a known location to a copy of an old photo or scan, but a scan has no original camera GPS to recover. Establish the place from reliable records, label uncertain locations as estimates in your archive, and keep the untouched scan.

You can geotag an old digital photo or scanned print if you can identify its location. The coordinates you add are an editorial assignment—not recovered camera data. Keep the untouched original, document how certain you are, and avoid guessing an exact point from a vague memory.

First decide what kind of “old photo” you have

  • Original digital camera file: it may already contain GPS. Inspect it before editing; a file downloaded from social media may be a stripped copy.
  • Scanned print or negative: the scanner records the scan process, not the original scene's location. Camera GPS cannot be recovered from a print unless it was separately recorded.
  • Photo with a location shown in an app: a library may store a manually assigned or estimated place separately from the exported image's EXIF. Check the file itself.

Use the EXIF viewer to inspect a candidate original. The EXIF GPS metadata guide explains the distinction between image metadata and information held elsewhere.

Build an evidence-based location estimate

Start with the strongest records you have: a dated itinerary, address book, letter, album caption, event program, property record, or a family member's specific recollection. Cross-check dates and names. A city-level identification supports a city-level archive label; it usually does not support an exact street address.

Write a small manifest beside the archive: filename, assigned place, coordinate source, confidence (confirmed / likely / uncertain), and who supplied the information. This preserves provenance when files are copied. Do not invent a capture date from scanner metadata: scanning time and photograph time are different.

Add coordinates to a working copy

  1. Duplicate the scan or camera file and retain the original in a read-only archive.
  2. Locate the best-supported point on a map. For a broad or uncertain location, choose a representative point only if your catalog clearly labels it as approximate.
  3. Open the geotagging tool, add the coordinates to the copy, and save with a filename that makes the edited version distinguishable.
  4. Reopen the exported copy with the GPS Finder and compare the displayed marker with your manifest.

For one known location across many scans, see the batch geotagging guide. Separate batches by actual place; do not assign one family-home coordinate to every image in an album just because they were stored together.

Do not silently replace camera capture time

GPS coordinates and capture timestamps are separate fields. A scanned image may have a current file-creation date or scanner-generated EXIF date. Leave that information intact unless you have a separate, reliable date and a tool that lets you edit the appropriate field. Document date corrections and their source.

Privacy and family archives

Exact coordinates can expose a private home, a living person's routine, or a sensitive cultural location. Consider keeping detailed tags in a private master and exporting a less precise or untagged copy for public albums. Ask before assigning a living person's home or a sensitive community site to a public image.

What an added location can and cannot do

A tag can make a private archive searchable and map-ready in software that reads the file's EXIF. It cannot authenticate the image, recover missing historical evidence, establish ownership, or prove that the photographer was physically at that point. If the location is uncertain, the honest label is more useful than a precise-looking but unsupported coordinate.

Frequently asked questions

Can I recover GPS from a scanned photo?

Not from the scan alone. A print contains no digital camera GPS fields; use separate records or recollections and mark any assigned location as an estimate.

Will geotagging change the original scan?

Work on a duplicate and retain the untouched scan; inspect the exported copy to confirm the change.

Is it okay to add an approximate location?

Yes, when you label its uncertainty in your archive and avoid implying it is camera-recorded or exact.
